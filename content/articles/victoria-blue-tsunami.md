Victoria has been firmly held by the ALP for 23 of the last 27 years. Tonight, however, this red streak will almost certainly be broken by an incoming blue tsunami accompanied by an orange wave. Now, Labor are predicted to retain only a dozen seats, losing relatively safe electorates to the Coalition, the Greens, and One Nation. When the current premier Ben Carroll was asked whether or not he would retain his seat of Niddrie, he claimed it was a “swing seat” despite being the local representative for Niddrie for 14 years and winning his election by twelve and a half points. It is almost guaranteed that Labor will lose, but there are still many outcomes for each individual electorate, as there are now four parties involved in this election with significant presence. But how did this happen? After all, Labor had managed to win the 2022 state election, despite the many criticisms of the government’s approach to COVID, locking down the state for 262 days, and yet they still managed to win 56 out of the possible 88 seats, securing an eleven-seat buffer. Also in 2022, it won the Two-Party-Preferred 55-45, and before this recent change, the polls were predicting a historic fourth consecutive term for the ALP. Despite this, there were early warning signs. In February of 2025, there was a by-election in the electorate of Werribee, where Labor’s primary vote dropped by almost 17 points. The support for Labor continued to decline as One Nation and the Coalition grew. Now, with Labor’s defeat almost ensured, there are really only two possible scenarios for this election: a coalition victory and a majority government, or a hung parliament, where the coalition must side with One Nation to form government, the first option being much likelier.

All the major polls at the moment predict a strong win for the Liberals, where the coalition should gain more than the sixteen seats it requires to win. Redbridge projects that Labor will get under 30 seats, ABC predicts Labor will lose 30-35 seats, whilst other websites predict even more carnage. Mustycaribou Forecasts predicts that when the dust settles, only 14 Labor seats will remain, just a quarter of the seats they had coming into the election. These numbers seem unfathomable. A party that almost won a fourth consecutive term could now face one of the biggest losses in Australian political history. But where are the regions where the right-wing parties are getting their gains?

Since 1999, Labor has never held fewer than eight of the 12 in and around Bendigo, Ballarat and Geelong. This election cycle, One Nation and the Liberals are very competitive in many of them, as the Liberals are likely to gain Bellarine, South Barwon and Geelong. In the South-East of Melbourne, electorates like Bentleigh and Carrum are likely to flip for the Liberals, with the coalition even looking likely to grab Frankston as well. The final area to be closely watched during this election is the home of the current Premier, Niddrie. If Carroll loses Niddrie, he will be the first Victorian Premier in almost 80 years to lose his seat.

If One Nation converts its votes into seats better than expected, they could pick up seats like Pakenham (the seat where the leader of One Nation for Victoria is going up for election), which is only being held by a slight Labor margin. A Roy Morgan poll suggested that One Nation could pick up Pakenham as well as Hastings and Morwell, along with the Coalition taking Bass. This could lead to a scenario where the Liberals do not pick up enough seats to form government, but this is unlikely.

With the scenario looking grim for Labor, they must realise they have no one to blame but themselves. They have driven Victoria into the ground over the past three decades, and they will pay the price. The state debt is over 160 billion, and the government has been lacking integrity. There is a Royal Commission investigating alleged corruption involving the CFMEU on major state infrastructure projects. Some may try to absolve Ben Carroll of the blame he will receive for the upcoming bloodbath. However, he was the minister for roads, Public Transport, Employment, youth crime, and corrections, Business Precincts and Education. It is clear that all three of the last Labor premiers have contributed to what will unfold on Election night; the only question now is how big that effect was.
